Akasia

Acacia auriculiformis

Note: The old labels on the trees say A. mangium, but the office guide and field features (yellow flowers, coiled ear-shaped pods) confirm A. auriculiformis. A. mangium has white or cream flowers and larger phyllodes (11–27 × 2.3–9.5 cm, with 3–4 main veins). Natural A. mangium × A. auriculiformis hybrids also occur in Malaysia, so an occasional tree may show intermediate features.

Family: Fabaceae (subfamily Mimosoideae)
Status: Not a mangrove. It's an introduced, invasive species native to northern Australia, Papua New Guinea and eastern Indonesia, usually found on the landward edge or on bunds.
English names: Earleaf acacia, northern black wattle
Name meaning: Acacia comes from Greek akis (thorn, point), an ancient name for a thorny tree. auriculiformis is Latin auricula (little ear) + forma (shape): "ear-shaped," from the coiled pods.

Identification

  • Habit: a tree up to 35 m tall, with fissured, grey to black bark (WATTLE).
  • What look like leaves are actually phyllodes: flattened leaf stalks that work as leaves. The tree has true leaves only at the seedling stage (NParks).
  • The phyllodes are curved like a sickle (falcate), 10–20 cm long and 1.2–3 cm wide, with 3 lengthwise veins (WATTLE).
  • Flowers: light golden-yellow, in spikes 5–8.5 cm long (WATTLE).
  • Pods: 3.2–16 cm long and 0.8–1.5 cm wide, leathery to woody, and strongly curved into an open coil (WATTLE). They start straight and twist into irregular spirals as they mature (World Agroforestry).
  • Seeds: dark brown to black, each encircled by a bright yellow funicle (the stalk that joins the seed to the pod) (WATTLE).

Ecology and adaptations

  • It forms a symbiosis with Rhizobium in root nodules, so it can fix atmospheric nitrogen. This lets it grow fast on poor, degraded soils such as ex-mining land and sandy areas.
  • It grows fast and tolerates fire, so it outcompetes native vegetation and can form dense stands.
  • Its pollen can trigger allergies and asthma in some people.

Uses (historical and current)

  • Planted widely as a shade, roadside and ornamental tree, for shelter on beaches, to stabilise eroded land, and to revegetate degraded land, including tin and bauxite mine tailings (PROSEA; Useful Tropical Plants). In a Malaysian trial on tin tailings, it grew best of the three tree species tested (Kamis Awang 1994).
  • Used for paper pulp, firewood, furniture and charcoal (Useful Tropical Plants). The bark contains 13–25% tannin and is collected locally for tanning (PROSEA).
  • Natural soap: the pods contain triterpenoid saponins (acaciasides) (StuartXchange). Crushed with water, they foam like soap. In its native Australia, Aboriginal people (Bininj Kunwok, Arnhem Land) crush the green pods to make soap (Bininj Kunwok plant knowledge), and rub the lather on to soothe itchy skin (StuartXchange).
    • Saponins are surfactants: one end of the molecule binds water and the other binds oil and dirt, so they lift grime off skin. This is the same principle as soap, but they are not chemically soap. True soap is a fatty-acid salt made by saponification. Saponins are glycosides made by the plant.
    • It does clean, but mildly. Saponins can sting the eyes and are toxic if swallowed in quantity. The same people use the crushed seeds as a fish poison (Bininj Kunwok plant knowledge).
  • More wood uses: turnery, toys, carom coins (buah karom), chess pieces, handicrafts, joinery and tool handles (Useful Tropical Plants).
  • Fuel: a very good firewood, and its charcoal glows well with little smoke and does not spark (Useful Tropical Plants).
  • Dye: a bark extract is used as a dye in Indonesia's batik industry (Useful Tropical Plants; PROSEA).
  • Soil and land: fallen phyllodes make a long-lasting mulch, and litterfall improves the soil. Seedlings can outcompete lalang (Imperata cylindrica) (Useful Tropical Plants).
  • Traditional medicine: a root decoction is used for aches, pains and sore eyes, and a bark infusion for rheumatism. Acacia bark in general is tannin-rich and astringent: it is taken internally for diarrhoea and dysentery, and used externally, often as a wash, on wounds (Useful Tropical Plants).

Field demo tip: Rub a fallen pod with a little water to show the foam. Make sure everyone washes their hands afterwards, and nobody touches their eyes or mouth. Never rinse the foam into a stream or pond, because it can harm fish.

Untuk adik-adik

"Pokok ni bukan pokok asal Malaysia tau. Dia datang dari Australia dan pulau-pulau berdekatan. Tengok buah dia yang jatuh atas tanah. Berpintal-pintal macam telinga! Daun dia pun sebenarnya bukan daun betul, tapi batang daun yang jadi leper, melengkung macam bulan sabit.

Kenapa orang bawa pokok ni ke sini? Sebab dia cepat sangat besar, dan boleh hidup di tanah yang tak subur, macam tanah bekas lombong. Akar dia ada 'kawan baik', iaitu bakteria kecil yang buat baja sendiri untuk pokok ni. Di Malaysia, orang pernah tanam dia di tanah bekas lombong bijih timah untuk hijaukan semula tanah yang botak. Ada juga orang tanam dia untuk jadi tempat teduh di tepi jalan, dan supaya tanah tak runtuh bila hujan.

Kayu dia pun berguna. Orang buat kertas, buat perabot macam meja dan kerusi, buat kayu api, dan buat arang. Kulit pokok dia pula ada bahan untuk mewarna dan menyamak kulit binatang.

Nak tengok satu magik? Akak/abang ambil buah akasia yang jatuh, letak sikit air, dan gosok-gosok. Tengok, keluar buih putih macam sabun! Dalam buah ni ada bahan semula jadi namanya saponin. Di Australia, tempat asal pokok ni, orang asli tumbuk buah muda dia untuk buat sabun, dan sapu buih dia pada kulit yang gatal. Tapi lepas ni kita basuh tangan ya, jangan sentuh mata atau mulut, sebab buih ni boleh buat mata pedih. Dan jangan buang dalam sungai, sebab dia boleh buat ikan mati!

Tapi sekarang dia jadi 'pokok pembuli', sebab dia rampas tempat pokok-pokok asal kita. Kawan-kawan yang ada lelah, hati-hati sikit, debunga bunga kuning dia boleh buat bersin dan sesak nafas.

Kayu akasia juga dibuat jadi buah karom, buah catur, mainan dan kraf tangan! Arang dia pula menyala lama, kurang asap dan tak memercik. Di Indonesia, kulit pokok ni dijadikan pewarna kain batik. Daun dia yang gugur jadi sungkupan dan baja untuk tanah, dan pokok ni boleh tumpaskan lalang. Ada juga orang rebus akar dia untuk ubat sakit badan dan sakit mata."

For adult groups

  • Why it's hard to remove: its hard-coated seeds build a long-lived soil seed bank, and fire stimulates germination. So cutting the trees alone rarely clears a stand. Follow-up weeding of seedlings is needed.
  • Hybrids: natural hybrids of A. mangium and A. auriculiformis occur, and have been formally named Acacia × mangiiformis. The hybrid is widely planted in Indonesia, Malaysia, Thailand, Vietnam and India, because it often grows faster, has a straighter trunk and tolerates drought better than either parent (Maslin et al. 2019).
  • Livestock caution (useful for orang kampung): many Acacia species can build up hydrogen cyanide, especially in drought, which can poison grazing goats and cattle. This is recorded for the genus, not tested specifically here.
  • Phyllodes are thought to reduce heat load and water loss compared with true leaves, part of why the tree thrives on hot, degraded land.
  • Lab findings: extracts have shown spermicidal activity in lab studies (StuartXchange). These are research results, not traditional uses.

Api-api Bulu

Avicennia rumphiana

Note: Malay names for Avicennia vary between sources. Follow Ecocare's signage.

Family: Acanthaceae (formerly placed in Avicenniaceae or Verbenaceae)
Status: True mangrove. A fast-growing pioneer of the seaward fringe and newly formed mudflats. Listed as Vulnerable (VU) on the IUCN Red List because it has a patchy distribution, is uncommon in parts of its range and is declining. It has been recorded in Setiu Wetland, Terengganu.
English names: No standard English name. English sources simply use "api-api"
Name meaning: Avicennia honours Avicenna (Ibn Sina, 980–1037), the Persian physician and philosopher. rumphiana honours Georg Eberhard Rumphius (1627–1702), the naturalist who catalogued the plants of Ambon in Herbarium Amboinense. Synonyms: in A. marina var. rumphiana, marina is Latin for "of the sea." In A. lanata, lanata is Latin for "woolly," from the hairy leaf undersides, the same idea as the Malay bulu.

Identification

  • Habit: a tree up to 30 m tall, with smooth, dark grey bark (NParks).
  • Pencil roots (pneumatophores): thin, upright roots poking out of the mud around the tree. They rise from long, shallow roots under the ground (NParks).
  • Leaves: opposite, ovate or elliptic, thick, up to 10 × 6 cm, on stalks about 18–20 mm long. The underside is covered in dense, powdery hairs (NParks) ("bulu" means hair or fur).
  • Flowers: orange-yellow and fragrant, in three-branched heads (NParks).
  • Fruit: broadly ovoid and flattened, up to about 18 mm long, covered in dense woolly hairs (NParks), shaped a bit like a kacang (bean or nut).

Lookalike: Api-api Putih (Avicennia alba, trail stop 9) has fruit that is egg-shaped with a broad base and a pronounced beak, up to 4 cm long (NParks). Api-api Bulu's fruit is smaller (up to about 18 mm), flattened and woolly. This is an easy field check when the trees are fruiting.

Adaptations

  • Pneumatophores have lenticels for gas exchange in anoxic mud. Even a tree only 2–3 m tall can have over 10,000, and bigger trees have more.
  • It is a salt secretor: salt glands on the leaves excrete excess salt, and salt crystals can sometimes be seen on the leaf surface. This contrasts with Rhizophora, which excludes salt at the roots by ultrafiltration.
  • Cable roots spread widely and bind soft mud, so it colonises and stabilises new mudflats.
  • Crypto-vivipary: the embryo germinates inside the fruit but does not break through the fruit wall before the fruit falls.

Uses (historical and current)

  • Planted for coastal defence and mangrove replanting (Giesen et al. 2006).
  • Malaysia: FRIM lists api-api bulu as a source of mangrove timber for construction, and its fruit as edible (FRIM 2020).
  • The wood is poor firewood, used mainly for smoking fish and other products, and rarely made into charcoal (Giesen et al. 2006).
  • Food: the seeds are boiled and eaten as a vegetable, and in some places sold as vegetables (Giesen et al. 2006).
  • More uses, recorded for the genus Avicennia (few records are specific to A. rumphiana):
    • Wood: house posts, columns, beams and roofing; boatbuilding; furniture and inlay; rice mortars (lesung) and mallets; pulp for rayon (PROSEA).
    • Smoke: the wood smoulders, which makes it useful for curing fish and smoking rubber sheets (PROSEA).
    • Ash: used as soap in India. In the Philippines, the wood is used to obtain salt (PROSEA).
    • Bark: used for tanning leather (though low in tannin), and on skin parasites and wounds (PROSEA).
    • Seeds: edible after roasting or boiling to reduce the bitter taste. Seed resin was used as an ointment for ulcers and tumours (PROSEA).
    • Leaves: leaves of A. marina (a different species) are used as green manure and fodder in the Middle East (PROSEA).

Untuk adik-adik

"Adik-adik, tengok tanah sekeliling pokok ni. Banyak 'pensel' kecil tercacak keluar dari lumpur kan? Itu bukan anak pokok tau, itu akar dia! Kita panggil 'akar pensel'. Akar ni macam straw untuk pokok bernafas, sebab lumpur ni kurang udara. Pokok yang tinggi 2 hingga 3 meter pun boleh ada lebih 10,000 akar pensel! Jadi jangan pijak ya.

Kenapa nama dia 'bulu'? Cuba pegang belakang daun. Berbulu halus, macam kain flanel! Daun dia juga pandai buang garam. Kalau cuaca panas, kadang-kadang kita boleh nampak kristal garam putih atas daun. Pokok ni 'berpeluh' garam!

Tengok buah dia. Bulat macam kacang, dan berbulu juga! Nanti kita jumpa kawan dia, api-api putih. Buah api-api putih pula macam telur, ada paruh di hujung.

Api-api bulu ni pokok yang berani. Dia antara pokok pertama yang tumbuh di lumpur baru tepi laut, dan akar dia pegang lumpur supaya tak dibawa ombak. Sebab tu orang tanam pokok ni untuk lindungi pantai.

Tapi pokok ni makin berkurangan, dan sekarang dia dalam senarai pokok dunia yang perlu dilindungi. Kita kena jaga dia baik-baik.

Di Malaysia, kayu api-api bulu digunakan untuk binaan. Buah dia pun boleh dimakan, tapi kena rebus dulu sebab rasa dia pahit. Jadi jangan cuba makan sendiri ya!

Kayu api-api tak sesuai untuk memasak, sebab dia terbakar perlahan dan berasap. Tapi itulah kelebihan dia! Ada orang guna asap dia untuk salai ikan dan salai getah keping. Kayu pokok keluarga api-api juga pernah dibuat lesung untuk tumbuk padi, tiang rumah dan perabot. Di India, abu kayu dia dijadikan sabun. Di Filipina pula, orang bakar kayu dia, siram dengan air laut, dan dapat garam! Ada juga orang sapu kulit pokok dia pada luka dan kudis."

For adult groups

  • Conservation: IUCN Vulnerable (2008 assessment), because of a patchy distribution and general decline. Any trees at Ecocare are worth protecting and documenting.
  • Taxonomy: it has been treated as A. marina var. rumphiana, and A. lanata is a synonym, so older records may use those names.
  • Salt secretion: salt glands in the leaf epidermis actively pump out Na⁺ and Cl⁻. Rhizophora takes the opposite approach and excludes salt at the root.
  • Sensitive traditional uses (genus Avicennia): seed resin was used as a contraceptive (PROSEA). The bark, roots and resin of A. officinalis were believed to be an aphrodisiac (PROSEA).
  • Drier fact: the wood is rated a weak fuel (PROSEA), and Rhizophora is the preferred species in most replanting programmes (PROSEA), which limits commercial interest in Avicennia.

Nyireh Bunga

Xylocarpus granatum · cannonball or puzzle-nut mangrove

Note: Usually skipped on staff tours. Other Malay names: nyireh, nireh, nyireh udang.

Family: Meliaceae (the mahogany family)
Status: True mangrove, on tidal mud, mostly towards the landward, upper part of the mangrove.
English names: Cannonball mangrove, puzzle nut, monkey puzzle mangrove
Name meaning: Xylocarpus is Greek xylon (wood) + karpos (fruit): "woody fruit." granatum means "full of seeds," as in the pomegranate (Punica granatum).

Identification

  • Habit: a tree up to 20 m tall (NParks).
  • Bark: smooth and flaky, light brown, yellowish or greenish. The trunk is grey with light brown to orange patches (NParks), so it looks blotchy like a camouflage uniform.
  • Roots: mature trees have long buttress roots that snake sideways along the ground. Young trees usually lack them (NParks).
  • Leaves: compound (pinnate), with up to 3 pairs of thick, leathery leaflets, 3.5–12 × 2–6 cm, with rounded tips (NParks). Along with Tuba Laut and Nipah, it is one of the few plants on the trail with compound leaves.
  • Flowers: small (about 0.8 cm), fragrant, with creamy-white or pinkish petals, in clusters of up to 3 on shoots up to 7.6 cm long from the leaf axils (NParks).
  • Fruit: a brown, woody capsule the size of a grapefruit to a small pomelo, like a cannonball. When ripe it splits into four parts (NParks).
  • Seeds: up to 20 per fruit, brown, up to 6 cm long, with a corky coat (NParks). Fitting the seeds back into their original shape is played as a puzzle game, hence "puzzle nut" (Stairway Foundation).

Ecology and adaptations

  • The corky seed coat makes the seeds float, so tides and currents disperse them. Seeds may start germinating while still floating, but it is not viviparous like the Rhizophoraceae.
  • Bees visit the flowers, and the flower shape suggests pollination by short-tongued insects.
  • The flowers are functionally unisexual, and some trees flower heavily but never fruit.
  • Damaged trees resprout from the base and recover well after their bark is stripped.

Uses (historical and current)

  • Timber: very hard, strong and durable, and it takes a high polish. It is a reddish-brown, mahogany-like wood used for tool handles, pins, house posts, boatbuilding and good-quality furniture (Useful Tropical Plants), and is valued for boats, houses and furniture (Wild Singapore). Crooked, often hollow trunks limit long straight pieces (Useful Tropical Plants). In Malaysia it is used for high-quality furniture, and FRIM also lists the wood for charcoal (FRIM 2020).
  • Bark tannin: 20–34% tannin. Used to strengthen ropes used in water (Useful Tropical Plants) and to tan and toughen fishing nets (Wild Singapore). It also dyes cloth brown or dark red (Useful Tropical Plants), and FRIM records its tannin as a cloth dye in Malaysia (FRIM 2020). The bark is thin, yet in one part of Java it was rare to find a tree that had not been peeled (Wild Singapore).
  • Seed oil: burned as lamp oil in India and used in some places as hair oil (Useful Tropical Plants). FRIM also lists the seed oil as lamp oil (FRIM 2020).
  • Food: the fruit peel is added to soups, and the dried peel is taken to whet the appetite (Useful Tropical Plants).
  • Fuel: gives great heat but burns very quickly (Useful Tropical Plants).
  • Traditional medicine: in Malaysia, the fruit and seeds are used for diarrhoea (FRIM 2020). The bark is used for dysentery, diarrhoea and fever (Useful Tropical Plants), and the roots in a "secret recipe" against cholera (Wild Singapore). Burned seeds mixed with sulphur and coconut oil are used for itchy skin, and the fruit for swellings (Useful Tropical Plants).

Untuk adik-adik

"Siapa nampak buah bulat besar ni? Macam bola meriam kan! Buah nyireh bunga boleh sebesar bola tampar, dan beratnya sampai 2 hingga 3 kilogram. Jadi jangan berdiri betul-betul bawah pokok ni bila buah dah masak ya!

Dalam buah ni ada sampai 20 biji, tersusun rapat macam teka-teki. Kalau dah terkeluar, susah nak susun balik! Sebab tu orang putih panggil dia 'puzzle nut'. Biji dia ada gabus, jadi boleh terapung dan belayar ikut air pasang.

Tengok kulit batang dia. Bertompok-tompok kelabu, perang muda dan oren, macam baju celoreng askar! Akar dia pula berliku-liku atas tanah macam reben.

Daun dia lain sikit dari pokok lain di sini. Satu daun ada beberapa pasang anak daun. Daun tua jadi oren kemerahan sebelum gugur.

Kayu nyireh keras, cantik dan berkilat bila digilap, macam kayu mahogani. Sebenarnya dia memang satu keluarga dengan pokok mahogani! Di Malaysia, kayu dia dibuat perabot yang cantik. Ada juga orang buat perahu, tiang rumah dan pemegang alatan daripadanya. Kulit pokok dia ada banyak tanin untuk kuatkan jala dan tali nelayan, dan untuk warnakan kain jadi perang atau merah gelap. Minyak dari biji dia boleh dijadikan minyak pelita, dan ada orang guna sebagai minyak rambut! Di sesetengah tempat, kulit buah dia dimasukkan dalam sup. Orang dulu guna buah dan biji nyireh untuk ubat cirit-birit. Ada juga orang guna kulit pokok dia untuk ubat cirit-birit dan demam."

For adult groups

  • Limonoids: Xylocarpus, like other Meliaceae, is rich in limonoids such as xylogranatins and xylocarpins. A recent study found anti-inflammatory activity in cell experiments (Trop. J. Nat. Prod. Res.). This is lab work, not a treatment.
  • Sensitive traditional uses: a decoction of crushed fruit was drunk as an aphrodisiac, and the fruit was used for swelling of the breasts and for elephantiasis (Useful Tropical Plants).
  • Breeding system: male flowers have a non-functional ovary and female flowers have sterile anthers. Some trees flower profusely but never fruit, which suggests the species is partly dioecious.
  • Bark harvesting: trees recover well after stripping, but the thin bark yields little per tree. The heavy peeling recorded in Java (Wild Singapore) is an example of a non-timber product being over-harvested.
  • Replanting: seeds germinate at about 70% in 4–10 weeks if sown convex side up, and direct sowing has worked in trial plantations.
  • Drier fact: the wood resists beetles but not termites.

Teruntum Merah

Lumnitzera littorea

Family: Combretaceae
Status: True mangrove of the landward/back mangrove zone, on firmer ground that is rarely flooded. Uncommon in many areas.
English names: Red-flowered black mangrove, red teruntum
Name meaning: Lumnitzera honours Stephan Lumnitzer (1750–1806), a German botanist. littorea is Latin for "of the seashore."

Identification

  • Habit: a small to medium tree, up to 25 m tall in favourable conditions (NParks).
  • Flowers: scented, with bright red petals, 1–3 cm long (NParks). Sources differ on where they grow: NParks places them in the leaf axils, while Raju et al. describe them at the branch tips (Raju et al. 2014, Taprobanica).
  • Leaves: obovate, 2–9 × 1–2.5 cm, slightly thick and fleshy, with a rounded or notched tip (NParks), arranged spirally in tufts towards the ends of the twigs (PROSEA).
  • Bark: dark brown and fissured (NParks).
  • Roots: no buttresses, but it has pneumatophores (NParks).
  • Fruit: vase-shaped, tinged red, 1–2 × 0.3–0.7 cm, with a fibrous wall that lets it float (NParks).

Lookalike: Teruntum Putih (Lumnitzera racemosa) has milky white flowers, in spikes 2–3 cm long in the leaf axils (Raju et al. 2014, Taprobanica).

Adaptations

  • Succulent leaves store water, which helps dilute salt.
  • Pneumatophores rise above the mud to take in oxygen in waterlogged soil (NParks 2025).
  • The red, tubular, nectar-rich flowers are adapted for bird pollination (ornithophily). Studies in Australia found honeyeaters to be the main pollinators. In Malaysia, sunbirds are the likely visitors.

Uses (historical)

  • Timber is heavy and fine-grained (Useful Tropical Plants), prized for its durability, especially in water. It was used for bridges, wharves, flooring and cabinet work (Giesen et al. 2006), and locally for boat building (Useful Tropical Plants). In Malaysia it is used as building material and firewood (FRIM 2020).
  • Also used for fuel (Useful Tropical Plants).
  • Ornamental: sometimes planted for its red flowers (Useful Tropical Plants).
  • Medicine: Useful Tropical Plants records no food or medicinal uses (Useful Tropical Plants), but FRIM notes that the leaves are said to be used for cleaning wounds (FRIM 2020).

Untuk adik-adik

"Tengok bunga merah menyala ni! Cantik kan? Bunga ni macam lampu isyarat merah untuk burung. Burung kelicap suka datang hisap madu dia, dan bila burung terbang ke bunga lain, dia bawa debunga sekali. Jadi burung tolong pokok ni buat buah!

Pegang daun dia. Tebal dan berair macam daun pokok kaktus, sebab dia simpan air dalam daun. Bentuk daun macam sudu kecil, ada lekuk sikit di hujung.

Pokok ni ada kawan, namanya teruntum putih, yang bunganya warna putih. Sebab tu kita panggil yang ni teruntum MERAH.

Kayu teruntum berat dan tahan lama, terutama dalam air. Di Malaysia, kayu dia digunakan untuk binaan dan kayu api, dan daun dia dikatakan boleh digunakan untuk cuci luka. Ada juga orang guna kayu dia untuk buat perahu, jeti, jambatan dan almari. Pokok ni tak banyak, jadi kalau jumpa, kita bertuah!

Sebab bunga dia cantik, ada orang tanam pokok ni sebagai pokok hiasan."

For adult groups

  • Pollination ecology: the red, nectar-rich flowers with long stamens are ornithophilous (bird-pollinated: honeyeaters in Australian studies, and likely sunbirds here). The white-flowered L. racemosa is pollinated by insects, mainly butterflies, bees and wasps. Where the two grow together, a natural hybrid, Lumnitzera × rosea, can occur (rosea means rose-coloured).
  • Family link: Combretaceae, the same family as ketapang (Terminalia catappa), which orang kampung will know.
  • Why it's uncommon: it grows towards the landward side of the mangrove, and in Sri Lanka its last small populations are under pressure from clearing for development (Madhushanka et al. 2022, Taprobanica).
  • Drier fact: Useful Tropical Plants records no food or medicinal uses (Useful Tropical Plants), and FRIM's only medicinal note is the leaves for cleaning wounds (FRIM 2020). Its value was mainly its timber.

Gambir Laut

Clerodendrum inerme, syn. Volkameria inermis · wild jasmine

Note: Usually skipped on staff tours. Ecocare's list uses Clerodendrum inerme. Newer references use Volkameria inermis.

Family: Lamiaceae (formerly placed in Verbenaceae)
Status: Mangrove associate, among shore vegetation and in the back mangrove.
English names: Wild jasmine, seaside clerodendrum, scrambling clerodendrum, Indian privet
Name meaning: Clerodendrum is Greek kleros (chance, fate) + dendron (tree): "tree of chance," said to refer to the unpredictable medicinal effects of the genus. inerme means "unarmed" (no thorns). The newer genus name Volkameria honours Johann Georg Volckamer the Younger (1662–1744), a German botanist. In Volkameria inermis, inermis is the same word as inerme, with the ending changed to match the feminine genus name.

Identification

  • Habit: an upright bush up to about 3 m tall, or a climber with stems up to 13 m long (NParks). It often grows as an untidy vine (Australian Tropical Rainforest Plants).
  • Leaves: opposite, thin (membranous), oval or narrowly lance-shaped, 3.8–8.8 × 1.8–4.3 cm (NParks), with tiny glandular dots on the underside (Australian Tropical Rainforest Plants).
  • Flowers: white or tinged purple, fragrant, in groups of 3–7 (NParks). Each flower is a long tube, 1.5–4 cm, with stamens 1.5–3.8 cm long sticking out like whiskers (Australian Tropical Rainforest Plants).
  • Fruit: drop-shaped, about 0.8 cm across, bright green turning black or brown (NParks), made of four nutlets that fit together (Australian Tropical Rainforest Plants).
  • No thorns: inerme means "defenceless, without spines or thorns" (NParks).

Ecology

  • Hawk-moth pollination: the flowers open in the evening and are strongly scented. The long corolla tube is hairy inside, which keeps out other insects. In a study in India, the hawk moth Macroglossum gyrans pollinated the flowers at dusk and dawn. Large bees took nectar without pollinating, acting as nectar robbers.
  • Protandry: each flower acts as male on its first day and as female on days 2–3, which limits self-pollination within a flower.
  • Birds disperse the fruits.
  • It tolerates salt spray and hard trimming.

Uses (historical and current)

  • Hedge and ornamental: tough and easy to trim, so it is commonly planted as a hedge, for example in India (Useful Tropical Plants). It is also planted on beaches to control erosion (Useful Tropical Plants).
  • Remedy for seafood poisoning: finely ground roots are used for upset stomachs caused by poisonous seafood, with a leaf decoction taken as a drink and used as a bath (Useful Tropical Plants). The seeds are used for this in Indonesia (PROSEA), and the seeds and roots for poisoning by fish and other marine animals (Wild Singapore).
  • Other traditional medicine: leaves on wounds and fruit for dysentery (Wild Singapore). A leaf poultice for skin diseases, swellings and itching (Useful Tropical Plants), recorded in Thailand (PROSEA). A root decoction for fever in the Philippines, and the leaves for fever in India (PROSEA). Pressed, the leaves give a thick, intensely bitter juice that is used against fever (Useful Tropical Plants). Root boiled in oil as a rub for rheumatism (Useful Tropical Plants). A decoction of roasted leaves for beriberi, recorded in Vietnam (PROSEA).
  • Food: in Indonesia the leaves are used in an appetiser dish (Wild Singapore).
  • Perfume: a fine perfume is said to be made from the flowers (Useful Tropical Plants).

For adult groups

  • Taxonomy: moved from Clerodendrum to Volkameria, and from Verbenaceae to Lamiaceae. Older species lists, including Ecocare's, use Clerodendrum inerme.
  • Breeding system: self-compatible but protandrous, with up to three flower forms (by stamen and style position) in one cluster. It's a good example of a mixed breeding system that still depends on a pollinator.
  • Lab findings: extracts are active against moulds and plant pathogens. Leaf extracts protected tobacco against tobacco mosaic virus, and showed insecticidal activity against the pulse beetle and the brown planthopper, a major rice pest. An extract also showed antiviral activity against hepatitis B in the lab (Useful Tropical Plants). These suggest potential as a botanical pesticide, not proven treatments.
  • Drier fact: apart from one Indonesian appetiser, it has no recorded food uses (Wild Singapore). The leaves contain a bitter principle (Useful Tropical Plants).

Buta-buta

Excoecaria agallocha

Family: Euphorbiaceae
Status: True mangrove of the back/landward mangrove and creek banks. It also tolerates lower salinity.
English names: Blind-your-eye mangrove, blinding tree
Name meaning: Excoecaria is from Latin excaecare, "to blind," because of the latex. agallocha is from Greek agallochon, agarwood (gaharu), because the wood was sold as a substitute.

Identification

  • Latex: a white latex oozes from any broken part (NParks). The latex, bark and twigs are poisonous. The latex is a strong irritant: on skin it can cause dermatitis, and in the eyes a painful inflammation (keratoconjunctivitis) and, in severe cases, blindness (Atlas of Poisonous Plants in Hong Kong). The genus name refers to this latex (NParks), and buta is Malay for blind. In English it is the "blind-your-eye mangrove" (Some Magnetic Island Plants).
  • Habit: a much-branched tree up to 15 m tall, with greyish-brown, warty bark (NParks).
  • Leaves: spirally arranged, oval to drop-shaped, 3.7–11 × 1.5–6 cm, papery and slightly fleshy, with shallowly toothed margins. There are 2–4 glands on each side of the base, where the blade meets the stalk. Old leaves turn bright red (NParks).
  • It is dioecious. The tiny yellow flowers grow on catkins in the leaf axils. Male catkins are 2–13 cm long, with scented flowers under 1 mm across. Female catkins are 0.5–3 cm long (NParks).
  • Fruit: an almost round, 3-lobed capsule, 4.5–5 × 8–9 mm, green turning brown, that splits to release 3 seeds (NParks).
  • Roots: shallow and surface-running, often knotted, with lenticels (NParks).

Adaptations

  • The latex is a chemical defence against herbivores.
  • The ripe capsule splits open to release its 3 seeds (NParks), and the seeds can also float. It is not viviparous.
  • Insect-pollinated. In some mangroves (e.g., the Sundarbans), it is an important nectar source for honeybees.

Uses (historical)

  • Latex was used as a fish poison, and crushed leaves put in water also stun fish (Useful Tropical Plants).
  • Latex and leaves were used, with great care, in traditional remedies (PROSEA).
  • Roots: used in traditional medicine for toothache and swellings (Some Magnetic Island Plants; PROSEA). The plant is toxic, so this is strictly historical.
  • The wood is soft, light and pale. It is used for matchsticks, carving and small implements (StuartXchange), and for planks, paper pulp, firewood and charcoal (Some Magnetic Island Plants). Across the genus, the wood is used for packing cases, toys and matches (PROSEA).
  • Incense: resinous pieces of the wood are used as a substitute for agarwood (gaharu) incense (PROSEA; Useful Tropical Plants).
  • More traditional medicine (all parts are poisonous, so all of these are strictly historical):
    • Latex: applied as a caustic to stubborn skin sores (Philippines) (PROSEA). Oil distilled from the wood or latex is applied to skin diseases (PROSEA), and the oil was used by Malays for itching and skin infections (StuartXchange).
    • Bark: chewing a small piece causes instant vomiting and purging, considered too drastic for constipation (PROSEA).
    • Resin: used against intestinal worms, for its purgative effect (Thailand) (PROSEA).
    • Roots: pounded with ginger and rubbed on swollen hands and feet (PROSEA).
    • Leaves: a decoction is given for epilepsy (PROSEA).
    • Juice: tiny amounts taken with coconut water for pneumonia or asthma (Papua New Guinea) (PROSEA).
    • Smoke from burning bark was used to treat leprosy (Some Magnetic Island Plants).
  • Hunting: the latex was added to ipoh (Antiaris toxicaria) sap to make dart poison (PROSEA).
  • Other: the roots are sometimes used as floats, and the tree is grown as an ornamental (Useful Tropical Plants).

Safety: Don't break leaves or twigs. If latex gets on skin, wash it off immediately. Never rub your eyes after touching the tree.

  • Dart poison: ipoh (Antiaris toxicaria) sap contains cardiac glycosides. Buta-buta latex was used as an additive to it (PROSEA).
  • Research: extracts have been studied in the lab and in animal models, with reported effects including pain relief (antinociceptive) and stomach protection (Turk. J. Pharm. Sci.). None are approved treatments.
  • Gaharu substitute: resinous pieces of buta-buta wood are used as a substitute for gaharu, and have their own local names, such as "garu laut" on Ternate, Indonesia (PROSEA).

Tuba Laut

Derris trifoliata · common derris

Note: Usually skipped on staff tours. Other Malay names: ketui, setui, akar ketuil.

Family: Fabaceae (the pea family, same as akasia)
Status: Mangrove associate, and one of the few climbers in the mangrove.
English names: Common derris
Name meaning: Derris is Greek for "leather covering" or "skin," referring to the tough, leathery pod. trifoliata means "three-leaved," although it has 3–7 leaflets (NParks).

Identification

  • Habit: a woody climber that can grow over 15 m long, sometimes creeping along the ground (NParks).
  • Leaves: pinnate, with 3–7 leaflets, despite the name trifoliata ("with 3 leaves") (NParks). The leaflets are leathery, egg- to lance-shaped or oval, 3–18 × 1.5–9 cm, with gradually narrowing tips. The end leaflet is slightly larger (NParks).
  • Flowers: pea-like, 9–12 mm long, cream-white to lavender or pinkish, in clusters (NParks).
  • Pods: coin-like: elliptic, disc- or egg-shaped, 3–5.5 × 2–3.7 cm, holding 1–3 flat, kidney-shaped seeds (NParks).

Ecology

  • Nitrogen fixation: root nodules with soil bacteria fix atmospheric nitrogen, like akasia.
  • Butterfly host plant: caterpillars of the Dark Caerulean butterfly feed on it.
  • Chemical defence: the roots, stems and pods are rich in rotenoids, including rotenone.

Uses (historical and current)

  • Fish poison (menuba): tuba is the Malay word for fish poison. The plant has been used traditionally as a fish poison, and the fish remain edible (Useful Tropical Plants; PROSEA).
  • Insecticide: used as an insecticide in Southeast Asia, but its action is weak, and it is not a source of commercial "derris dust" (Useful Tropical Plants), which comes mainly from a relative, Derris elliptica (Giesen et al. 2006).
  • Fibre: stem fibres are made into ropes and fishing nets (Useful Tropical Plants; Giesen et al. 2006).
  • Tannin: the bark contains up to 9.5% tannin (Useful Tropical Plants).
  • Fodder and ornamental: leaves are sometimes fed to cattle and other livestock. It may suit parks, growing over trellises and pergolas, for its pea flowers and coin-like pods (NParks).
  • Traditional medicine: roots as a stimulant and counter-irritant, and for rheumatism (India). In Papua New Guinea, a root decoction is applied to the skin for fever and drunk for sores. Roots or stems as a laxative and for arthritis (Thailand) (PROSEA). A leaf decoction for malaria (Papua New Guinea) (Useful Tropical Plants).

Nama dia 'tuba' sebab pokok ni boleh digunakan untuk 'menuba' ikan. Bila pokok ni dimasukkan dalam air, bahan racun dalam dia, namanya rotenon, buat ikan pening dan terapung, jadi senang ditangkap. For adult groups

  • Rotenone toxicology: rotenone blocks mitochondrial complex I. WHO classes it as "moderately hazardous." It is only mildly toxic to mammals but extremely toxic to fish and insects, because it is absorbed easily through gills and tracheae but poorly through skin or the gut. It breaks down in sunlight, usually within about six days. Human deaths are rare because it tends to cause vomiting, but deliberate ingestion can be fatal (Useful Tropical Plants).
  • Parkinson's link: Betarbet et al. (2000, Nature Neuroscience) showed that chronic rotenone exposure in rats reproduced key features of Parkinson's disease (Betarbet et al. 2000). Rotenone is now a standard laboratory model of the disease.
  • The law: under Malaysia's Fisheries Act 1985, section 26(1)(a), using any explosive, poison or pollutant to catch fish is an offence.
  • Sensitive traditional uses: roots for painful menstruation (India). In Papua New Guinea, the outer bark was soaked in water or in the liquid of a green coconut, then squeezed, and the juice was drunk to prevent infection, especially by new mothers and by people who had handled corpses or the body fluids of the sick (Useful Tropical Plants).
  • Drier fact: it has only weak insecticidal action and is not a source of commercial derris dust (Useful Tropical Plants), which comes mainly from D. elliptica (Giesen et al. 2006).

Bebaru

Hibiscus tiliaceus, syn. Talipariti tiliaceum · sea hibiscus

Family: Malvaceae
Status: Mangrove associate, found on the landward fringe and along beaches and riverbanks.
English names: Sea hibiscus, beach hibiscus, coast cottonwood
Name meaning: Hibiscus is from Greek hibiskos, an ancient name for the marsh mallow. tiliaceus means "like Tilia" (the linden or lime tree), because of the similar heart-shaped leaves. The synonym Talipariti was created by Paul Fryxell (2001) when he split this group from Hibiscus. It is generally explained as coming from tali-pariti, the Malayalam name for this tree recorded in Rheede's Hortus Malabaricus (1678–1693). Parutti means cotton, which belongs to the same mallow family. tiliaceum is the same word as tiliaceus, with the ending changed to match the neuter genus name.

Identification

  • Habit: a small, shrubby tree up to 15 m tall, with a spreading crown (NParks).
  • Leaves: heart-shaped, with a pointed tip and finely toothed margins, spirally arranged (NParks). The blades are 5–30 cm long, and the underside is whitish and densely covered in star-shaped (stellate) hairs (Plants of South Eastern NSW).
  • Flowers: large and funnel-shaped, bright yellow with a purple eye (NParks), with petals 4–9 cm long (Plants of South Eastern NSW). Each flower opens at around 9am, turns dull orange and dull pink in the afternoon, and drops at around 4pm the same day (NParks).
  • Fruit: a rounded, densely hairy capsule about 2–2.5 cm long (Australian Tropical Rainforest Plants), brown when ripe, that splits into 5 parts to release many small, kidney-shaped seeds (NParks). The seeds float for many weeks, which suggests they spread by water (hydrochory) (Australian National Botanic Gardens).

Lookalike: Thespesia populnea is also called "baru" or "baru-baru" in Malaysia (PROSEA). It has hairless, more fleshy leaves without teeth, and yellow flowers with a yellow eye (NParks).

Uses (historical)

  • The inner bark gives a strong bast fibre, used for rope and for caulking boats (Useful Tropical Plants; Giesen et al. 2006).
  • The light, soft wood was used for net floats and corks, light construction and carving (Useful Tropical Plants).
  • Medicine: the leaves are used for coughs and sore throats, and a treatment made from the leaves, roots and bark for fever (Useful Tropical Plants). In Malaysia, the roots are used to treat fever (FRIM 2020).
  • Tempeh starter: in Indonesia the leaves are used to make usar, a traditional tempeh starter: Rhizopus mould is grown on soybeans sandwiched between waru (bebaru) leaves, and its spores are then used to start tempeh (Nout et al. 1992, J. Sci. Food Agric.).
  • More fibre uses: the rope is stronger when wet. The fibre is also used for mats, sandals, slings and paint brushes, and the bark is beaten into tapa bark cloth (Useful Tropical Plants).
  • More wood uses: Polynesians used it for canoe outriggers, fishing rods, fishnet frames, handles and tools. Dry wood is used to make fire by friction, and as firewood for slow smoking. The pulp only makes wrapping paper (Useful Tropical Plants).
  • Food: the flowers are eaten raw or cooked as a potherb. The leaves taste lemony, and are fermented into a sauce or boiled in salted water to make a drink called onge tea. Young leaves and the green bark were eaten in times of famine, and the mucilage-rich bark was sucked. The leaves are also used to grow tempeh starter culture (Useful Tropical Plants).
  • Seeds: in Malaysia, the seeds are processed into oil, perfume and an ointment for skin and hair problems (FRIM 2020).
  • More traditional medicine: a leaf poultice for sores, cuts, wounds, boils and swellings. Bark and leaves for coughs, sore throats and tuberculosis. Crushed flowers on skin abscesses, and the flowers as a laxative. Bark for eye injuries and stomach-ache. In Fiji, leaves are wrapped around fractures and sprains, and the leaves are also used to help childbirth (Useful Tropical Plants).
  • Planting: used as a living fence, hedge, windbreak (Hawaii) and shade tree, and for coastal rehabilitation and erosion control. Also grown as an ornamental (Useful Tropical Plants; Giesen et al. 2006).

Api-api Putih

Avicennia alba

Note: Usually skipped on staff tours. Compare it with Api-api Bulu (trail stop 2).

Family: Acanthaceae
Status: True mangrove. A pioneer on newly formed mudbanks on the seaward side and along rivers. IUCN Least Concern.
English names: No standard English name. English sources simply use "api-api putih"
Name meaning: Avicennia honours Avicenna (Ibn Sina). alba is Latin for "white," from the white leaf underside, matching the Malay putih.

Identification

  • Habit: a medium to large tree up to 20 m tall (NParks).
  • Leaves: oblong to lance-shaped and pointed, about 15 × 5 cm, glossy green above and very pale underneath. Both putih and alba mean white, referring to this pale underside (NParks).
  • Bark: smooth, brownish black (NParks).
  • Roots: a wide system of side roots just under the surface, with thin pencil pneumatophores 15–20 cm tall (NParks).
  • Flowers: yellow to orange, 3–4 mm across, in clusters (NParks).
  • Fruit: pale green, egg-shaped with a broad base and a pronounced beak, up to 4 cm long (NParks).

  • Salt secretion through glands on the leaves, like api-api bulu.
  • Crypto-vivipary: a seedling forms as soon as the fruit drops. Seedlings have hooked hairs that make them cling together in tangled clumps while floating.
  • As a pioneer, it colonises bare mud and areas where other mangroves have been cut.
  • Leaf and fruit shapes vary a lot in shade or poor conditions, so check several leaves before deciding.

Uses (historical and current)

  • Timber: moderately hard wood with all-round uses: construction, poles, posts, furniture, boatbuilding and decorative items (Useful Tropical Plants).
  • Fuel: poor firewood, occasionally made into charcoal (Useful Tropical Plants).
  • Resin: collected and used for various medical purposes (Useful Tropical Plants). In Malaysia, resin from the seeds is used for skin diseases (FRIM 2020), and seed resin is made into an ointment for skin disease and wounds (Giesen et al. 2006).
  • Food: in Malaysia, the seeds are eaten after cooking (FRIM 2020), and the seedlings are cooked as a vegetable (Giesen et al. 2006).
  • Traditional medicine: the heartwood is used to treat thrush (a fungal mouth infection) in children (PROSEA).
  • Restoration: as a natural pioneer, it could help re-establish mangroves on exposed coasts (Useful Tropical Plants).
  • Uses recorded for the whole genus Avicennia (smoking fish and rubber, lesung, salt-making, ash as soap) are listed under Api-api Bulu.

For adult groups

  • Conservation contrast: A. alba is Least Concern while A. rumphiana is Vulnerable. Mangrove area within A. alba's range has still declined by an estimated 24% since 1980, mainly from extraction and coastal development.
  • Zonation: Avicennia and Sonneratia are typical seaward pioneers. The classic zonation scheme by inundation class was developed from Malayan mangroves (Watson 1928).
  • Identification caution: shade, low nutrients and stress produce non-standard leaf and fruit shapes, so a single odd leaf can mislead.
  • Sensitive uses recorded for the genus (seed resin as a contraceptive, A. officinalis as an aphrodisiac) are in the Api-api Bulu box.
  • Drier fact: it is considered poor firewood, only occasionally used for charcoal (Useful Tropical Plants).

Bakau Kurap

Rhizophora mucronata

Family: Rhizophoraceae
Status: True mangrove. Typically found along creek banks and the seaward to mid zone.
English names: Loop-root mangrove, Asiatic mangrove
Name meaning: Rhizophora is Greek rhiza (root) + phoros (bearing): "root-bearing," from the stilt roots. mucronata is from Latin mucro (sharp point): "with a short sharp tip," describing the leaf tip.

Identification

  • Very prominent stilt or prop roots arching from the trunk. Aerial roots sometimes hang from the lower branches (NParks; World Agroforestry).
  • Terms: akar jangkang are the stilt roots that arch out from the trunk. "Prop root" is used loosely. This page says "stilt or prop roots" for these trunk roots, but some sources, such as a 2007 study that describes R. stylosa producing "prop roots from lower branches" (Mohd Nasir & Safiah Yusmah 2007, JTFS), and Ecocare staff keep "prop roots" for the aerial roots that drop from the branches. So when someone says "prop root," check which one they mean.
  • Habit: an upright tree 20–25 m tall (NParks).
  • Bark: almost black or reddish, rough or sometimes scaly, with horizontal cracks (World Agroforestry; NParks). Unconfirmed: the name "kurap" (ringworm) may refer to this scaly look; no source found.
  • Leaves: broadly elliptic to slightly oblong and leathery, 11–23 cm long, on stalks 2.5–5.5 cm long (NParks). The stalks are green (Ngernsaengsaruay et al. 2024, PeerJ). The tip ends in a stiff point (mucro) more than 3 mm long (Ngernsaengsaruay et al. 2024, PeerJ), up to 0.8 cm, but often broken off (Gillikin & Verheyden, Kenyan mangrove guide). The underside has many conspicuous tiny black cork warts (Ngernsaengsaruay et al. 2024, PeerJ).
  • Flowers: 2–5 per cluster on a long stalk (NParks), or 3–9 in Thailand (Ngernsaengsaruay et al. 2024, PeerJ). The 4 white petals, about 9 mm long, have densely hairy margins, and the calyx is pale yellow (NParks).
  • Propagule: cylindrical, rough and warty, 36–64 cm long and up to 2 cm wide (NParks); 25–70 cm in Thailand (Ngernsaengsaruay et al. 2024, PeerJ).

How to tell it from Bakau Minyak (R. apiculata): R. apiculata has smaller leaves (7–19 × 3.5–8 cm) with reddish leaf stalks (NParks), and flowers in pairs, without individual stalks (PROSEA).

Adaptations

  • Prop roots give mechanical support in soft mud and have lenticels for aeration.
  • The roots exclude salt by ultrafiltration at the root membranes.
  • Vivipary: propagules drop and either spear into the mud or float until they find a place to establish.
  • The tangled roots trap sediment, slow currents and act as a nursery habitat for fish, prawns and crabs.

Uses (historical and current)

  • Produces high-quality charcoal and firewood (Useful Tropical Plants). The Matang Mangrove Forest Reserve in Perak has been managed for Rhizophora charcoal and poles under plans going back to 1904 (Mongabay 2024). The reserve is mostly R. apiculata and R. mucronata, and its charcoal kilns are in districts such as Kuala Sepetang (Wolswijk et al. 2022, Remote Sensing). FRIM lists bakau kurap as firewood (FRIM 2020).
  • Used for heavy construction, poles and piling, and its bark tannin is used to toughen and dye the lines, nets and ropes of fishermen (Useful Tropical Plants).
  • More wood uses: fish traps and house frames. It is an excellent fuel, valued especially for baking, and splits easily. A report from Guam says the wood is durable in water and underground, suiting the foundations of bridges and wharves (Useful Tropical Plants).
  • More tannin uses: tanning leather. Tannin is concentrated into cutch, and used as a deep brown or black dye. Ash left after extraction can be used as fertiliser. Lab work suggests the tannin could make plywood adhesive (Useful Tropical Plants).
  • Food: according to a report from Guam, the fruit is occasionally eaten: it is first boiled, then wood ash is added to neutralise the bitter taste, and the fruit is then baked (Useful Tropical Plants).
  • Traditional medicine: used occasionally for blood in the urine (haematuria), probably the bark (Useful Tropical Plants).
  • Replanting: wild seedlings are collected for planting (Useful Tropical Plants).

About tannin

Rhizophora bark is among the richest sources of tannin in mangroves. Keep the tree's own functions separate from human uses:

  • For the tree (chemical defence):
    • Antimicrobial and antifungal, which protects tissues and slows decay.
    • Deters herbivores: tannin binds proteins, making tissue bitter, astringent and hard to digest.
  • Human uses (Useful Tropical Plants):
    • Tanning leather (awet kulit haiwan): tannin cross-links the collagen in animal hide, turning it into durable leather.
    • Dye: deep brown or black.
    • Fishing nets, lines and ropes: toughened and dyed with the bark, the classic mangrove use.

Tengar Merah

Ceriops zippeliana

Note: Confirmed by Ecocare staff (7 Oct 2026) that the species on the tour is Ceriops zippeliana. Staff call it "tengar," but its more precise name is "tengar merah." Ceriops tagal (also called "tengar") is not present around Ecocare.

Family: Rhizophoraceae
Status: True mangrove, found in the middle to landward zone.
English names: No standard English name. The genus Ceriops is sometimes called "yellow mangrove" or "spurred mangrove"
Name meaning: Ceriops is Greek keras (horn) + opsis (appearance): "horn-like." zippeliana honours Alexander Zippelius (1797–1828), a Dutch botanist who collected in Indonesia. The older name decandra (see the taxonomy note below) is Greek deka (ten) + anēr, andros (man, here meaning stamen): "ten stamens."

Taxonomy note (useful for you): C. zippeliana was long treated as a synonym of C. decandra. Sheue et al. (2009) used morphological and molecular evidence to show it is a distinct species. It is the name for the Southeast Asian populations, including Malaysia.

Identification

  • Habit: a small tree, up to 10–12 m tall (NParks).
  • Bark: greyish brown with protruding lenticels, flaky at the base (NParks).
  • Roots: small stilt roots at the base of the trunk (NParks). NParks gives no measurements.
  • Leaves: opposite, oval to oval-elliptic, 5.5–11 × 3–7.5 cm, on stalks 1–2.5 cm long, with a blunt tip (NParks).
  • Flowers: 3–5 per cluster, at the top three nodes of a branch, with 4 white petals that soon turn brown. The 5 calyx lobes stand erect while the flower is open (NParks).
  • Propagule: with angular ridges, gradually thickening to a sharp point, 8–17 cm long. Look for the characteristic red collar below the calyx (NParks), an easy way to remember the "merah."

Adaptations

  • Vivipary, like the other Rhizophoraceae on the tour.
  • Stilt roots with lenticels give support and aeration in soft mud.

Uses (historical)

  • Malaysia: FRIM records that tannin from tengar bark and wood is used as a dye, and that the wood is used for building, firewood and good charcoal (FRIM 2020).
  • Ceriops bark is very rich in tannin (about 20% to over 40%). It is used to preserve and dye fishing nets and matting, and gives red to brown dyes. In central Java, Ceriops bark (called "tingi") is one ingredient of the traditional soga-batik dye (PROSEA).
  • More uses, recorded for the genus Ceriops (mostly C. tagal, since few records are specific to C. zippeliana):
    • Traditional medicine: the bark was used locally in traditional medicine in Peninsular Malaysia and Indonesia (PROSEA). In tropical Africa it is used as an astringent, to stop bleeding, and as a substitute for quinine against malaria. It has also been used for diabetes, and in lotions for ulcers and abdominal ailments (Useful Tropical Plants).
    • More tannin uses: tanning, preserving sails as well as nets, and as a binder for particle board (Useful Tropical Plants).
    • More wood uses: in Africa, small planks for boatbuilding, paddles and oars, and fishing stakes, and in Kenya, wattlework for walls and roofs. Also tool handles and pulp (Useful Tropical Plants).
    • Fuel: in many places prized as firewood, second only to Rhizophora (Useful Tropical Plants). It makes excellent charcoal, but in some areas it is said to burn so hot that it damages cooking pots (PROSEA).

Berus

Bruguiera cylindrica

Note: Confirmed as Bruguiera cylindrica in Ecocare's plant list. In mangrove terminology, "berus" refers to Bruguiera. A related species, B. hainesii, is called "berus mata buaya."

Family: Rhizophoraceae
Status: True mangrove. It is often a pioneer on stiff, poorly drained clay in the middle zone.
English names: Reflexed orange mangrove, black mangrove
Name meaning: Bruguiera honours Jean Guillaume Bruguière (1750–1798), a French naturalist. cylindrica means "cylindrical," describing the smooth, round propagule.

Identification

  • Habit: a medium to large tree up to 20 m tall, with short buttresses and knee roots, bent like knees, that rise above the mud. The bark is greyish (NParks).
  • Leaves: opposite, oval, 6–17 × 2–8 cm, thinly fleshy and leathery, shiny green above and light green below (NParks).
  • Flowers: hanging, in small clusters of about 3 in the leaf axils. The calyx is light green, 4–6 mm long, with 8 pointed lobes, and the petals are white and hairy, 3–4 mm long. The calyx lobes bend back as the seed germinates (NParks).
  • Viviparous propagules: cylindrical, short and thin, 8–15 × 0.8 cm, turning from green to purple when mature (NParks).
  • Lookalike: Tengar Merah (trail stop 11) propagules are angular, with ridges you can feel (NParks). Berus propagules are cylindrical (NParks). See the table in the Tengar Merah section.

Adaptations

  • Knee roots have lenticels for gas exchange in anoxic, waterlogged sediment.
  • Vivipary: the embryo germinates while still attached to the parent tree, so the seedling is ready to establish quickly when it drops.
  • Bruguiera flowers have an explosive pollination mechanism. The petals hold pollen under tension and release it onto insects that probe the flower.

Uses (historical)

  • Firewood and charcoal: in Malaysia, firewood and charcoal are more important uses of Bruguiera than timber (PROSEA). FRIM lists berus as firewood (FRIM 2020), and B. cylindrica stands are harvested for charcoal and chipwood (PROSEA).
  • Poles: Bruguiera timber is used for piles, poles, fishing stakes and tool handles (PROSEA).
  • Tannin: the bark of the related B. gymnorhiza is widely used for tanning leather and fishing nets (PROSEA).
  • Traditional medicine: in Malaysia, FRIM records the stem for treating ulcers and stopping bleeding (FRIM 2020). Across Bruguiera, the fruit is used for shingles and eye diseases, and the roots and leaves for burns (PROSEA). The bleeding use fits the chemistry: tannins are astringent. They bind proteins and tighten tissue, helping blood clot at small wounds, the same way an astringent styptic works. This would have been for minor cuts, not serious haemorrhage.
  • Food: young shoots are eaten raw as a salad, and the young propagules are occasionally boiled and eaten as a vegetable or made into a preserve, mainly in times of famine (Useful Tropical Plants, citing PROSEA). Across Bruguiera, the leaves are sometimes boiled and eaten too, and a condiment can be obtained from the bark (PROSEA). In some areas, young embryo roots are eaten with sugar and coconut (Giesen et al. 2006). Across Bruguiera, the fruit has also been chewed as a substitute for betel nut (pinang) (PROSEA).
  • Fish traps: fishermen avoid the wood for fish traps, because it is said to repel fish (Giesen et al. 2006).

For adult groups

  • Silviculture: very slow growing. It needs a rotation longer than 20 years and only reaches exploitable size at about 40 years, so it suits charcoal or chipwood rather than sawn timber (PROSEA).

Thrombolytic means clot-dissolving, which is the opposite of stopping bleeding, a nice illustration of why lab results and traditional uses need separate testing.
  • FRIM's view: FRIM describes berus as an excellent source of antioxidants for traditional medicine (FRIM 2020). This is a general statement, not a tested treatment.
  • Pollination: berus is insect-pollinated. Its flowers release a cloud of pollen when an insect probes the base. The larger-flowered tumu merah (B. gymnorhiza) is recorded as pollinated by birds and insects, and possibly wind.

Nipah

Nypa fruticans

Family: Arecaceae (palm family)
Status: True mangrove, and the only mangrove palm. Grows along brackish riverbanks and estuaries where salinity is lower, often forming dense stands.
English names: Nipa palm, mangrove palm
Name meaning: Nypa is a Latinised form of the local name nipa (nipah) used in the Malay world and the Philippines. fruticans is Latin for "shrubby," since the plant has no upright trunk.

Identification

  • No upright trunk. Nipah is a clumping palm whose stem creeps underground as a rhizome (NParks). A rhizome is a stem, not a root: it grows sideways under the ground and produces shoots and roots from its nodes (Gale Encyclopedia of Science).
  • Fronds: pinnate, 4–10 m long, standing up from the ground, with up to 100 leaflets on each side. Each leaflet is 60–130 × 5–8 cm (NParks).
  • Inflorescence: upright, on a stalk 0.9–1.7 m long that rises from the underground stem. The female flowers form a round head about 6–8 cm across, with catkins of male flowers below it. The flowers are orange to yellow. Because male and female flowers are on the same plant, nipah is monoecious (NParks).
  • Fruit: brown to dark brown fruits packed into a round cluster. Each is a fibrous, one-seeded fruit, 8–11 × 6–8 cm, with an angular tip, dispersed by water (NParks).

Adaptations and interesting facts

  • The rhizome anchors the plant in soft mud.
  • Seeds can begin germinating while still in the fruit cluster (vivipary).
  • Pollinated mainly by small flies.
  • Ancient lineage: fossil Nypa pollen and fruits go back to the Late Cretaceous (around 70 million years ago). Fossils are even found in Europe (e.g., the London Clay), showing it was far more widespread when the climate was warmer.

Uses (historical and current)

  • Food: the sweet, jelly-like endosperm of young seeds is eaten raw as a snack (PROSEA; Useful Tropical Plants). As "buah atap" (attap chee), the young seeds are eaten as a sweetmeat and used to flavour ice cream and local ice desserts in Malaysia (Tsuji et al. 2011, Sains Malaysiana).
  • Sap (nira nipah): tapped from the cut flower stalk (PROSEA; photos of the tapping: JKKN Pemetaan Budaya). Fresh nira is sweet and non-alcoholic, and in Malaysia it is boiled into gula nipah (palm sugar) in family enterprises (PROSEA). In Sarawak, nipah sugar is called gula apong (also spelt gula apung) (Fong 1989, Principes). The sap carries its own wild yeasts and spoils quickly (Chongkhong & Puangpee 2018, Songklanakarin J. Sci. Technol.). Left standing, it ferments: the yeasts convert the sugars to ethanol, and acetic acid bacteria then oxidise the ethanol into acetic acid (Rastina et al. 2026, Biodiversitas, a study of kabung (Arenga pinnata) sap). Nipah sap is made into tuak or toddy, and into cuka nipah (vinegar) (Tsuji et al. 2011, Sains Malaysiana). Nipah sap was studied as a source of fuel alcohol in Malaysia and the Philippines (PROSEA).
  • Leaves: made into atap thatch in Malaysia, the Philippines, Indonesia and Thailand (PROSEA), and woven into walls (Useful Tropical Plants). Leaflets and midribs are made into baskets, mats and sunhats (Useful Tropical Plants; photo: Berita Harian). In Malaysia, the young leaves were used for rokok daun (hand-rolled cigarettes) (FRIM 2020).
  • Leaflet midribs (lidi): made into penyapu lidi (brooms) (Useful Tropical Plants; photo: Inspirasi Alang).
  • More food: the flower cluster is cooked in its own syrup to make an energy-giving sweet (Useful Tropical Plants).
  • Leaf stalks (pelepah): strong, with many structural uses, and made into arrows. The leaves contain up to 10% tannin (Useful Tropical Plants).
  • Traditional medicine: juice from young shoots against herpes. Ash of burned nipah for toothache and headache. Also used for centipede bites (Useful Tropical Plants).
  • Planting: planted along swampy coasts and mudflats for erosion control (Useful Tropical Plants).

Daun nipah dianyam jadi atap dan dinding rumah. Sebab tu kita panggil 'rumah atap'! Daun juga dibuat bakul, tikar dan topi. Lidi dari anak daun dijadikan penyapu lidi untuk sapu sampah. Dulu, daun nipah muda juga digunakan untuk gulung rokok daun, tapi sekarang kita tahu merokok sangat bahaya untuk kesihatan ya!

Ada orang masak tandan bunga nipah dalam sirap nira jadi manisan yang memberi tenaga. Pelepah nipah yang kuat ada banyak kegunaan, dan pernah dijadikan anak panah! Ada orang bakar bahagian pokok nipah dan guna abunya untuk ubat sakit gigi dan sakit kepala. Nipah juga pernah jadi ubat bila digigit lipan. Orang tanam nipah di tepi sungai dan pantai supaya tanah tak terhakis."

For adult groups

  • Nipah virus myth-buster: the virus is named after the Malaysian village of Kampung Sungai Nipah. It was found in 1999 among workers exposed to pigs, and its natural reservoir is fruit bats (flying foxes, Pteropus) (Henry 2019, Emerging Infectious Diseases). The palm has nothing to do with the virus.
  • Tuak: fermentation of nira is spontaneous and fast. Some visitors may be sensitive about alcohol, so judge the group before going into detail.
  • Fuel alcohol: the sugary sap was studied as a source of fuel alcohol in the Philippines and Malaysia in the 1920s, and in Papua New Guinea in the 1980s, where it was not economically feasible (PROSEA).
  • Palaeoclimate indicator: fossil Nypa pollen is used as a marker of past tropical climates, for example in Eocene deposits such as the London Clay.
  • Floating nipah islands (local observation): a small floating island carrying a clump of living nipah palms, with dead fronds around its base, was photographed from the Ecocare jetty on 27 Oct 2026. It moves: its position on the river changes from day to day, and it sometimes drifts so far that it can only be reached by boat (observed at Ecocare, Oct–Nov 2026). On 16 Nov 2026 it was gone from the spot in the 27 Oct photo. That it floats fits the spongy leaf stalks, which are light enough to be used as fishing-net floats (Tsuji et al. 2011). Unconfirmed: what keeps the island afloat (a mat of rhizomes and dead fronds, or trapped mud and debris), and whether it moves with each tide or mainly with river flow and wind.
  • Branching rhizome: the rhizome shows true dichotomous branching: its growing tip divides into two equal shoots (Tomlinson 1971, Annals of Botany). This is very rare in flowering plants. Most palms that branch do so from side buds, and many, like coconut, never branch at all. It is how one nipah plant spreads into a clump along the riverbank.

Bakau Minyak

Rhizophora apiculata

Family: Rhizophoraceae
Status: True mangrove, found in the middle zone on deep, soft mud that is regularly flooded. It is the most important commercial mangrove species in Malaysia and the main species in the Matang Mangrove Forest Reserve.
English names: Tall-stilt mangrove
Name meaning: Rhizophora means "root-bearing" (see Bakau Kurap). apiculata is from Latin apiculus, "with a small, abrupt point," describing the leaf tip.

Identification

  • Habit: a tree up to or over 30 m tall, with a trunk up to 50 cm across and dark grey bark (NParks).
  • Stilt or prop roots (see Terms under Bakau Kurap), arching from up to 5 m up the stem. Aerial roots sometimes hang from the branches, as in Bakau Kurap (NParks).
  • Leaves: elliptic, 7–19 × 3.5–8 cm, dark green, smooth and leathery (NParks), with an acute to apiculate tip (PROSEA): a stiff point under 3 mm long in Thailand (Ngernsaengsaruay et al. 2024, PeerJ), but about 4–5 mm in a study in the Andaman Islands (Ragavan et al. 2015, PhytoKeys). Older or dried leaves show minute, scattered black cork warts underneath (PROSEA).
  • Leaf stalks and stipules: the leaf stalks are reddish, 1.5–3 cm long (PROSEA), and long red stipules grow at the leaf bases (NParks). In Thai plants the stalks are usually green, with the top pair red or green (Ngernsaengsaruay et al. 2024, PeerJ).
  • Flowers: in pairs, sessile (without individual stalks), on a thick stalk 0.5–1.5 cm long, growing from leaf scars below the leaf rosette. The flowers are yellow, and the petals are hairless (PROSEA).
  • Propagule: cylindrical to club-shaped, dark green or purplish dark green, glossy, 25–45 cm long. The tube at the top, where it joins the fruit, is red, dark greenish red or reddish dark green (Ngernsaengsaruay et al. 2024, PeerJ).
  • At this stop: the large Bakau Minyak here shows both root types: big stilt roots from the trunk, and a long aerial root hanging from a branch, not yet reaching the ground (seen on the trail, Oct 2026). In the related R. stylosa, roots from the lower branches grow into the soil and help support the tree's weight (Mohd Nasir & Safiah Yusmah 2007, JTFS). Unconfirmed (Ecocare staff): staff estimate the tree is about 40 years old.

Name notes

  • "Minyak": One explanation is that from a distance, across the water, the glossy leaves look oily. This is a common explanation, not a confirmed origin, so present it as "salah satu penjelasan."
  • "Bakau merah": Some people call it this because of the red parts: the reddish collar at the top of the mature propagule, and the reddish petioles. Note that the red collar is the cotyledons, not the shoot (pucuk). Treat "bakau merah" as a local name. In English, "red mangrove" usually means R. mangle of the Americas, and some sources also apply it to R. mucronata.

Adaptations

  • Same as Bakau Kurap: stilt roots for support and aeration, salt exclusion by ultrafiltration, and vivipary.

Uses (historical and current)

  • One of the main species used for charcoal in Malaysia (FRIM 2020). In the Matang reserve, Rhizophora is harvested on a 30-year rotation for charcoal and poles, and then replanted (Mongabay 2024). More than 80% of Matang's charcoal is exported to Japan (Behara et al. 2021, Frontiers in Ecology and Evolution).
  • Poles are used for piling (cerucuk), construction and fishing stakes, and the wood is in great demand as firewood (PROSEA). FRIM lists it for cerucuk in Malaysia (FRIM 2020; photo: Pintas Trading).
  • Bark tannin is used for tanning leather and to toughen and dye fishing lines, ropes and nets (PROSEA).
  • More uses: in Matang, logs from 30-year-old stands are made into charcoal in kilns, with charcoal factories in districts such as Kuala Sepetang, Perak (Wolswijk et al. 2022, Remote Sensing). In Thailand it is grown in plantations mainly for charcoal (Hassan et al. 2018, BioResources), and in firewood plantations (PROSEA). It has been harvested for wood chips in East Malaysia and Indonesia (PROSEA), and FRIM notes its potential for furniture (FRIM 2020).
  • Most other recorded Rhizophora uses (leather tanning, cutch, fish traps) apply to both species. See Bakau Kurap.

For adult groups

  • Matang scale: the Matang Mangrove Forest Reserve covers about 40,000 ha (Mongabay 2024). R. apiculata is the preferred species in most replanting programmes in Southeast Asia (PROSEA). Charcoal is made in kilns in districts such as Kuala Sepetang (Wolswijk et al. 2022, Remote Sensing).
  • Hybrid: Rhizophora × lamarckii is a natural hybrid of R. apiculata and R. stylosa. Natural Rhizophora hybrids have been identified with DNA markers in Peninsular Malaysia (Ng et al. 2013, Tree Genetics & Genomes).
  • Debate: Matang is a sustained-yield success, but studies have found lower bird diversity in production patches. A 2024 study concluded the system is only sustainable if its conservation zones are respected (Mongabay 2024).

Dungun Laut

Heritiera littoralis · looking-glass mangrove

Note: Ecocare's list and pamphlet use "Dungun Laut." Staff and many sources also just say "dungun" or "pokok dungun" for this species.

Family: Malvaceae (formerly Sterculiaceae)
Status: True mangrove of the landward/back mangrove zone, where tidal flooding is rare.
English names: Looking-glass mangrove
Name meaning: Heritiera honours Charles Louis L'Héritier de Brutelle (1746–1800), a French botanist. littoralis is Latin for "of the shore."

Identification

  • Habit: a tree up to 35 m tall, with whitish-grey to pinkish-grey bark (NParks).
  • Leaves: spirally arranged, leathery and stiff, 6.3–30 × 3.8–15 cm. The underside is scaly silvery white to brown (NParks). The silvery scales reflect strong sunlight (Green Power).
  • Roots: prominent plank or ribbon buttresses at the base (NParks).
  • Flowers: dull purple and bell-shaped, about 6.3 mm wide, in clusters 5–18 cm long in the leaf axils. Male and female flowers are separate but on the same tree (monoecious) (NParks).
  • Fruit: woody, glossy brown or purple, 3.8–8 × 3–6 cm, with a keel on one side. It can float for weeks (NParks).

Adaptations

  • Plank roots give stability on soft soil and help with aeration.
  • The silvery scales may reduce water loss and reflect strong sunlight.

Uses (historical)

  • Dungun timber is hard, strong and durable (Useful Tropical Plants), and is traded as "dungun" timber (PROSEA). In Malaysia it is used for construction (FRIM 2020). It is used for building ships, boat-ribs, masts, bridges, furniture and house-posts (NParks).
  • It is excellent firewood, with a high energy value (PROSEA).
  • The bark contains tannin, used to toughen fishing nets (PROSEA).
  • Seeds: in Malaysia, the fruit and seeds are used for diarrhoea (FRIM 2020), and a seed extract is used for diarrhoea and dysentery (PROSEA). The seeds are occasionally eaten (PROSEA).
  • Kayu sugi (chewing stick): the twigs are used as toothbrushes (Useful Tropical Plants; NParks), though neither source says where. The end of a twig is chewed until it frays into bristles, then used to scrub the teeth. Unconfirmed (Ecocare staff): staff say dungun twigs were used this way locally, as kayu sugi.
  • More wood uses: ships' masts when straight and long enough, joists, wheel hubs, boat ribs, furniture and rice pounders (antan). Recommended for steam-bent woodwork. The pulp is suitable for wrapping, writing and printing paper (Useful Tropical Plants).
  • More traditional medicine: in Kenya, a root decoction is used for mouth infections and toothache (PROTA).
  • Food: the seed has been used as a substitute for cola nuts (Useful Tropical Plants).
  • Fish poison: in the Philippines the roots are used as a fish poison (PROSEA).
  • Ornamental: grown as an ornamental and street tree, though its buttress roots can make it unsuitable (Useful Tropical Plants).

For adult groups

  • Seed oil chemistry: rich in cyclopropenoid fatty acids (malvalic acid about 54%, sterculic acid about 12.5%). These are anti-nutritional, and in animal studies they disrupt fatty-acid metabolism.
  • Bioactive compounds: a root compound, vallapin, has shown activity against boll weevils (a cotton pest). The roots are also used as a fish poison (PROSEA).
  • Zonation indicator: a back-mangrove species, typical of the landward zone that is only flooded by the highest tides.
  • Name of Dungun district: Dungun, the district next to Kemaman (where Kertih is), is said to be named after this tree, because the area was once a swamp full of dungun trees (Toponymy Heritage Places of Malaysia). This is the popular account. The source gives no evidence for it, and the origin of the earliest settlement is unclear.
  • Drier fact: its buttress roots can make it a poor street tree (Useful Tropical Plants).

Pandan Laut

Pandanus tectorius, syn. P. odoratissimus · screw pine

Family: Pandanaceae (a monocot, so not a "true" tree in the woody dicot sense)
Status: Mangrove and beach associate, found on sandy and landward coastal areas.
English names: Screw pine, thatch screwpine, hala (Hawaii)
Name meaning: Pandanus is a Latinised form of the Malay word pandan. tectorius is Latin for "of roofs" (from tectum, roof), because the leaves are used for thatching. The synonym odoratissimus is the Latin superlative "most fragrant," from the strongly scented male flowers.

Identification

  • Habit: a large, branching shrub or small tree up to 14 m tall, with sharp conical prickles on the trunk (NParks).
  • Leaves: strap-shaped, 80–180 cm long and 4–8 cm wide, in spiral clusters at the branch tips, with sharp spines along the edges and under the midrib (NParks). The spiral arrangement gives the name "screw pine" (Columbia Encyclopedia).
  • One-way spines: the prickles on the leaf margins, up to 4 mm long, point towards the leaf tip (antrorse) (Plants of Hawaii). A hand sliding from base to tip passes smoothly, but sliding from tip to base hooks the skin. Checked by hand on the Ecocare trail (Oct 2026). The small prickles under the midrib can point either way (Plants of Hawaii), so never rely on this to handle the leaf.
  • Roots: many prop and stilt roots from the base of the trunk and the side branches (NParks), like the bakau.
  • It is dioecious (separate male and female plants). Male flowers are small, white and fragrant, with a large white bract. Female plants produce a pineapple-like compound fruit (syncarp), 8–30 cm long and 4–20 cm wide, that ripens from green to orange (NParks).
  • The fruit breaks into wedge-shaped segments (phalanges, or "keys") that are fibrous and buoyant (NParks).

Ecology

  • It tolerates salt spray, wind and sandy soil, and helps stabilise the coastline.

Uses (historical)

  • Leaves are woven into hats, mats, baskets, clothing and sails, and used for roofing (Useful Tropical Plants). In Malaysia, pandan and mengkuang leaves are woven into mats, sacks, hats and baskets, though the source does not say which species (UiTM 2026). In Setiu, Terengganu, pandan laut leaves are woven into mats, prayer mats (sejadah) and baskets, but only two people in the district still make them commercially (Utusan Malaysia 2026, with photos).
  • The fragrant male flowers give an essential oil used in perfumery (Useful Tropical Plants).
  • The fruit is eaten in some Pacific islands, and the roots and leaves have traditional medicinal uses (Useful Tropical Plants).
  • Not the same as cooking pandan (Pandanus amaryllifolius).
  • More food: in parts of the Pacific the fruit is a staple food. Forms low in calcium oxalate crystals are eaten raw; others must be cooked to destroy the crystals. The fruit is made into juice, cooked into jam, or dried and made into flour, a paste or thin, flat cakes. The seeds taste nutty, raw or cooked. The terminal buds and the inner base of young leaves are eaten raw or cooked as a vegetable, the flower buds are eaten as a vegetable, and the aerial roots are cooked and made into a drink (Useful Tropical Plants).
  • Fragrance: the male flowers are distilled into kewra water, used to flavour desserts and drinks. Their essential oil goes into perfumes, cosmetics, hair oils and soaps, and the flowers are used to scent coconut oil (Useful Tropical Plants).
  • More fibre uses: the roots make skipping ropes, basket handles and a black weaving dye. Prop roots are used as string and as wall slats. Aerial root tips are beaten into brushes, and dried fruit segments are also used as brushes (Useful Tropical Plants).
  • Trunk and resin: trunks are used for houses, ladders, canoe masts, fishing rafts and water pipes. A resin from the trunk is used as glue and for caulking boats (Useful Tropical Plants).
  • Fuel: dried fruit segments are slow-burning, so they are preferred for barbecues (Useful Tropical Plants).
  • More traditional medicine: a root decoction for haemorrhoids. Juice of young roots on fish bites and wounds. Leaves for colds, flu, asthma and boils (Useful Tropical Plants).
  • Planting: dune stabilisation, windbreaks, living fences and mulch. Also grown as an ornamental (Useful Tropical Plants).

  • Food security: on Pacific atolls with poor soils, it's a staple food crop, with many named cultivars (Useful Tropical Plants).
  • Cosmetics: a fruit extract is used commercially as a humectant (moisturiser) (Useful Tropical Plants).

Perepat

Sonneratia alba · mangrove apple

Note: Usually skipped on staff tours. Another local name is bedada.

Family: Lythraceae (formerly placed in Sonneratiaceae)
Status: True mangrove. A pioneer of the seaward fringe, especially on sandy mudflats in sheltered estuaries. IUCN Least Concern.
English names: Mangrove apple
Name meaning: Sonneratia honours Pierre Sonnerat (1748–1814), a French naturalist and explorer who travelled in Southeast Asia. alba is Latin for "white," from the white petals and stamens.

Identification

  • Habit: a tree with a broad, spreading crown, usually 15–20 m tall and sometimes up to 30 m (NParks).
  • Bark: cream-coloured to brown, with fine lengthwise fissures (NParks).
  • Roots: thick, blunt, cone-shaped pneumatophores up to 1 m tall (NParks), much thicker than api-api pencil roots.
  • Leaves: opposite, elliptic to ovate or obovate, with a rounded or broad tip, 5–11 × 4–8 cm, on stalks 6–15 mm long (NParks).
  • Flowers: a powder puff of many white stamens, which soon fall. The white petals are thread-like, 13–20 × 0.5–1.5 mm, and easy to miss. The flowers grow in clusters of 5–8 and open only at night (NParks). Each is 4–6 cm across, and the calyx is green outside and red inside (CIRAD mangrove checklist).
  • Fruit: a hard, fleshy berry, round and flattened, 2–4.5 cm across, ripening green, with the calyx still attached at the base (NParks). The calyx lobes bend back (reflexed) (CIRAD mangrove checklist).

Ecology and adaptations

  • Night flowers: they open at dusk, smell of sour milk or butter, and last only one night.
  • Bat pollination: nectar bats are the main pollinators, drinking from the calyx cup. Hawk moths visit where bats are scarce, and birds feed on the nectar at dawn and dusk.
  • Pneumatophores supply oxygen to the roots in waterlogged mud.
  • It does not tolerate long exposure to fresh water.
  • Heavy sedimentation buries the pneumatophores and kills the tree, so it is a poor choice for replanting on very silty sites.
  • It binds soil, reduces erosion and shelters the land behind it.

Uses (historical and current)

  • Pneumatophores: used as a cork substitute (PROSEA), and made into floats and the wooden soles of shoes (Useful Tropical Plants). Papuans make corks and floats from them (Wild Singapore).
  • Food: the ripe fruit is eaten by the Malays and in Java, and used to flavour fish in the Moluccas (Wild Singapore). FRIM lists the fruit as edible in Malaysia (FRIM 2020). It is eaten raw or cooked, is slightly acid, used to make vinegar, and is said to taste like cheese. The leaves are eaten raw or cooked (Useful Tropical Plants).
  • Timber: moderately heavy, hard and durable, and resistant to shipworm. Used for boats, canoes, piling, bridges, house posts, paddles, masts, and door and window frames (Useful Tropical Plants), as well as wharves, furniture and musical instruments. Bronze nails are needed (Wild Singapore), because salt in the wood corrodes iron (PROSEA).
  • Fuel: gives a lot of heat but also a lot of ash and salt, so it is inferior firewood. It is also used for charcoal (Useful Tropical Plants). In Malaysia, the stems and branches are used as firewood (FRIM 2020).
  • Bark: 9–12% tannin and a brownish dye (Useful Tropical Plants). In Malaysia, FRIM records a natural purple dye from the bark (FRIM 2020).
  • Traditional medicine: the fruit in a compress to stop bleeding (Wild Singapore). Used for cuts and bruises, and the fruit for intestinal worms and coughs (Useful Tropical Plants).

For adult groups

  • The durian connection: the dawn bat (Eonycteris spelaea) feeds on Sonneratia flowers and is a key pollinator of durian and petai. Mangroves are part of the food supply that sustains these orchard pollinators. This is a strong argument for mangrove conservation with orang kampung.
  • Fireflies: the famous synchronised flashing of Pteroptyx tener, as in Selangor, is linked to berembang (S. caseolaris), not perepat.
  • Restoration caution: it is poorly suited to replanting where sedimentation is high, because sediment buries the pneumatophores. Its seeds also lose viability within about three months, though it can be propagated by air layering.
  • Wood chemistry: the wood may resist marine borers, but its salt corrodes iron spikes and bolts (PROSEA).
  • Drier fact: its bark has only 9–12% tannin (Useful Tropical Plants), far less than tumu merah's 20–43% (Useful Tropical Plants).

Tumu Merah

Bruguiera gymnorhiza · large-leafed orange mangrove

Note: Usually skipped on staff tours. Compare it with Berus (trail stop 12), a close relative.

Family: Rhizophoraceae
Status: True mangrove of the middle to landward zone, on firmer, better-aerated soil, often alongside Rhizophora. It is one of the largest and longest-lived mangrove trees, occasionally reaching 36 m. IUCN Least Concern.
English names: Large-leafed orange mangrove, oriental mangrove, orange mangrove (Australia), black mangrove (South Africa)
Name meaning: Bruguiera honours Jean Guillaume Bruguière (see Berus). gymnorhiza is Greek gymnos (naked) + rhiza (root): "naked root," from the exposed knee roots.

Identification

  • Habit: a tree up to 20 m tall in Singapore (NParks), generally 8–25 m and occasionally 35 m, with a trunk 40–90 cm across and a buttressed base (World Agroforestry). It has many knee roots (NParks).
  • Bark: dark grey to brown and rough, with distinct lenticels (NParks).
  • Leaves: opposite and long-stalked, thinly fleshy and leathery, oval to lance-shaped or broadly oval, 9–24 × 4–9 cm, dark green above and light green below (NParks).
  • Flowers: hanging, one per leaf axil, on a short stalk. The calyx is pinkish to dark red, 1.5–2.5 cm long, top-shaped, opening into 10–16 pointed lobes. The 9–14 petals are white to creamy orange, 1.5–2.5 cm long and hairy, with 3–4 bristles at the tip (NParks).
  • Propagule: cigar-shaped, long, stout and ridged, 19–25 × 1–1.7 cm, ripening from green to dark purple. It grows out of the calyx (NParks).

Adaptations

  • Knee roots with lenticels for aeration, plus buttresses for support.
  • Vivipary: seedlings can float alive in water for 5–6 months.
  • Explosive pollination: the petals form pouches holding loose pollen under tension. When a bird probes the flower, the pouch "unzips" and scatters a cloud of pollen over the bird's head. Insects also visit.

Uses (historical and current)

  • Food: in Malaysia, the leaves and fruit are eaten after boiling (FRIM 2020). The propagules are eaten after boiling and scraping, "but not when there is anything better available" (Wild Singapore), and in the Moluccas, in times of scarcity, the leaves and peeled propagules are soaked in water to remove the astringent tannins, then boiled and eaten (Useful Tropical Plants). In Indonesia the inner flesh is cooked with sugar into a sweet (Wild Singapore). The fruits are sometimes sold as a vegetable in local markets, and are used in betel quid when nothing better is available (Useful Tropical Plants). The leaves are eaten raw as a flavouring (Wild Singapore).
  • Condiment: the bark is used to flavour raw fish, though eating too much is said to be dangerous (Wild Singapore).
  • Tannin and dye: very rich in tannin (20–43% of the dry bark), used to preserve fibres in ropes and sails (Useful Tropical Plants), and to treat fishing lines (Wild Singapore). The bark gives orange to reddish-brown dyes, which turn darker with iron-rich mud (Useful Tropical Plants).
  • Timber: heavy, strong, hard red wood for foundation piles, mine timbers, house posts and furniture (Wild Singapore), and rafters, fishing stakes and telegraph poles (Useful Tropical Plants). FRIM describes the wood as hard and durable, and suitable for wood pulp (FRIM 2020).
  • Fuel: a very good firewood and charcoal (Useful Tropical Plants; Wild Singapore).
  • Traditional medicine: bark as an astringent for diarrhoea, and sometimes for malaria (Cambodia) (Wild Singapore). Bark applied to burns. Fruit as an eye medicine. A root decoction with Piper leaves for bleeding, diabetes and high blood pressure (Useful Tropical Plants).
  • Planting: planted along coasts for protection from sea spray, storm surges and saltwater intrusion, and could be used for reforestation (Useful Tropical Plants).

Tumu merah juga antara pokok paya bakau yang paling tinggi dan paling lama hidup. Ada yang boleh sampai 36 meter tinggi, lebih tinggi dari bangunan 10 tingkat!

For adult groups

  • Sensitive traditional use: it has been used as an abortifacient (Useful Tropical Plants).
  • Tannin record: up to about 43% of the dry bark, among the highest of any mangrove. Phlobaphene pigments in the bark give black and dark-brown dyes (Useful Tropical Plants).
  • Fuel value: the wood's energy value is about 19,000–20,000 kJ/kg (Useful Tropical Plants).
  • Management concern: natural regeneration is usually good, but regeneration after felling is often poor or absent.
  • Pollination: recorded as pollinated by birds and insects, and possibly wind.
  • Taxonomy: formerly called B. conjugata.
  • Context: Least Concern, but mangrove area within its range has declined by an estimated 20% since 1980.

Bakau Lilin

Kandelia candel · narrow-leaved kandelia

Note: Large mature trees, with propagules, grow on the riverbank by the Ecocare car park near the entrance gate. ID confirmed by Ecocare staff (Oct 2026). "Bakau lilin" is the name used at Ecocare. Published Malay names include lingayong, bakau aleh-aleh and beus (PROSEA), and berus-berus, beras-beras, pulut-pulut and pisang-pisang laut (Ngernsaengsaruay et al. 2026, Biodiversitas). Don't confuse "berus-berus" with Berus (Bruguiera cylindrica).

Family: Rhizophoraceae
Status: True mangrove, on the banks of tidal rivers among other mangroves (PROSEA), more often in the back mangrove (NParks). Rare in Southeast Asia (Giesen et al. 2006). IUCN Least Concern globally, but rare in Thailand (Ngernsaengsaruay et al. 2026) and Critically Endangered in Singapore (NParks). Nearby, it was among seven species recorded in a 2006 survey of mangroves at Tok Bali (Kelantan) and Kemaman (Terengganu), though the record does not say at which site (KUSTEM 2006).
English names: Narrow-leaved kandelia
Name meaning: Kandelia and candel both come from "Tsjerou-kandel," the name used in van Rheede's Hortus Malabaricus (1686), which Linnaeus cited when he named the species (Ngernsaengsaruay et al. 2026). So "candel" is not the English word "candle," though the Malay lilin does mean candle.

Identification

  • Habit: a shrub or small tree, about 7–9 m tall, with a thickened stem base (NParks).
  • Roots: no stilt roots and no pneumatophores. The roots sometimes look braided at the trunk base (NParks).
  • Bark: greyish to reddish-brown, smooth, with lenticels (NParks).
  • Leaves: opposite, elliptic-oblong, 6–13 × 2.5–6 cm, on petioles 1–1.5 cm long, with the margin curled inward (NParks).
  • Flowers: white and bisexual, 1.5–2 cm long, in forked clusters of 4–9 (NParks). Each of the 5 petals is deeply split, and each half ends in a fringe of thread-like segments. There are 32–46 stamens (Ngernsaengsaruay et al. 2026).
  • Propagule: green, smooth, slender and club-shaped, 15–40 cm long, sometimes tinged red (NParks); 35–43.5 cm in Thai populations (Ngernsaengsaruay et al. 2026).

How to tell it from the trail's Rhizophoraceae: Bakau Kurap, Bakau Minyak and Tengar Merah have stilt roots, and Berus and Tumu Merah have knee roots. Bakau Lilin has neither, and its white flowers have fringed petals.

Adaptations

  • Vivipary, like the other Rhizophoraceae: the propagule grows on the tree before it drops (PROSEA).
  • In Thailand, flowering, fruiting and germination on the tree go on almost all year, peaking from November to March (Ngernsaengsaruay et al. 2026).

Taxonomy note

Until 2003, the Kandelia of East Asia (southern China, Taiwan, Japan) were included in K. candel. They are now a separate species, K. obovata (Useful Tropical Plants), which includes Japan's mehirugi (メヒルギ) (Japan Tourism Agency). K. obovata has obovate leaves with fewer side veins (Sheue et al. 2003, Taiwania) and much shorter propagules, 15–20 (–23) cm (Ngernsaengsaruay et al. 2026). Older books that say K. candel grows in Japan or Hong Kong mean K. obovata.

Uses (historical)

  • Wood: fuelwood, charcoal and temporary construction (PROSEA).
  • Bark: about 17% tannin, used for tanning heavy leather and dyeing red and brown (PROSEA), and for strengthening fishing nets (Ngernsaengsaruay et al. 2026).
  • Farming: used as green manure (PROSEA) and livestock fodder (Ngernsaengsaruay et al. 2026).
  • Traditional medicine: in India, the bark is used for diabetes, together with dried ginger or long pepper and rosewater (Ngernsaengsaruay et al. 2026).

  • Pokok ni jarang dijumpai di Asia Tenggara, jadi pokok-pokok besar di tepi sungai dekat tempat letak kereta ni sangat berharga.
  • Anak pokok dia tumbuh masa masih tergantung atas pokok, panjang dan halus macam lilin hijau.

  • Ada orang guna kulit pokok dia untuk mewarna jadi merah dan perang, dan untuk menyamak kulit binatang.
  • Nama sains dia, candel, datang dari nama lama di Kerala, India, bukan dari perkataan Inggeris "candle".

Ketapang

Terminalia catappa · Indian almond

Note: Found at the campsite (tapak perkhemahan), which is also the emergency assembly point, and behind the jetty by the main hall (dewan utama).

Family: Combretaceae (the same family as Teruntum Merah)
Status: Mangrove associate. A beach forest tree that often grows on the landward fringe of mangroves (Giesen et al. 2006). It naturally grows on sandy or rocky beaches and is native from India through Southeast Asia (PROSEA).
English names: Indian almond
Name meaning: Terminalia is from Latin terminus (end), because the leaves cluster at the ends of the twigs. catappa comes from the Malay name ketapang (NParks).

Identification

  • Habit: a deciduous tree 10–25 m tall, occasionally 35 m, often buttressed (PROSEA).
  • Branches: horizontal and in tiers 1–2 m apart, giving young trees a pagoda shape (PROSEA).
  • Leaves: large, obovate, usually 15–25 × 8–15 cm (up to 38 × 24 cm), shiny, clustered at the branch tips (PROSEA). The tree sheds its leaves twice a year, and they turn red before they fall (NParks).
  • Flowers: very small, greenish-white, on spikes 8–16 cm long. Most are male, and they have no petals (PROSEA).
  • Fruit: 3.5–7 cm long, slightly flattened with a keel, green turning yellow or red, with a thin layer of juicy flesh around a hard stone (PROSEA).

Ecology

  • Tolerates salty soil and sea spray, and is very wind-resistant (PROSEA).
  • Seeds are spread by sea water and by fruit bats, and stay viable for a long time (PROSEA).
  • Biting and stinging ants have been found living in its hollow twigs (Useful Tropical Plants).

Uses (historical and current)

  • Food: the seed is edible and tastes like almond, which gives the tree its English name (NParks). It yields a pale oil similar to almond oil. The fruit flesh is edible but often fibrous (PROSEA). The seeds are eaten raw or roasted, or chopped into biscuits, bread, desserts, sweets, soups and stews (Useful Tropical Plants).
  • Timber: good-quality wood for house and boat building, furniture and cabinet-making, though termites attack it (PROSEA).
  • Tannin and dye: the bark has 11–23% tannin. Bark and leaves tan leather and give a black dye for cotton, rattan and ink (PROSEA).
  • Shade tree: planted along avenues and in gardens for its pagoda shape (PROSEA).
  • Traditional medicine: the leaves are applied to rheumatic joints, and in the Philippines a leaf decoction is used against worms. The tannin-rich bark is used as an astringent for dysentery and thrush (PROSEA).
  • Fish keeping: in a 2016 study, ketapang leaf extract added to the water improved the survival of betta (ikan laga) kept in tanks (Nugroho et al. 2016, Biosaintifika).

For adult groups: whole-tour points

  • Blue carbon: Donato et al. (2011) found mangroves store on average about 1,000 tonnes of carbon per hectare, up to four times more than most other tropical forests. Between 49% and 98% of it is in the waterlogged soil, so clearing mangroves releases carbon that would otherwise stay locked away.
  • Main threats in Malaysia: conversion to aquaculture ponds and agriculture, coastal development, and pollution.
  • English names are unreliable: "black mangrove" is used for berus, for tumu merah in South Africa, for teruntum merah ("red-flowered black mangrove") and for Avicennia in the Americas. With researchers, always use the scientific name.
  • Sustainable use is possible: Matang shows mangroves can be harvested for a century without disappearing, if they are managed and replanted.
  • Acid sulfate soils: when mangroves are cleared and drained, for example for shrimp ponds, the pyrite in the black mud (see below) meets air and oxidises to sulfuric acid. The resulting acid sulfate soils are one reason many ponds built on former mangrove land have failed (Dent 1986).

Why is the mud black underneath?

Dig a little into the brown surface mud and it turns black and smells of rotten eggs.

  • Brown on top: the top few millimetres to centimetres get oxygen from the air at low tide and from the water. Iron in the mud oxidises to brown and orange iron oxides, like rust.
  • No oxygen below: the fine, waterlogged mud blocks oxygen, and microbes near the surface use up what does arrive.
  • Sulfate-reducing bacteria: without oxygen, these bacteria use the sulfate in seawater to break down dead leaves and roots, releasing hydrogen sulfide (H₂S): the rotten-egg smell (Kristensen et al. 2008).
  • The black colour: H₂S reacts with iron to form black iron monosulfide (FeS), and some of it slowly turns into pyrite (FeS₂). Partly decayed plant material darkens the mud further. The line between the brown and black layers is the redox boundary (redox potential discontinuity, RPD).
  • Things to show: brown linings around crab burrows and roots, which carry oxygen down into the black layer. A lump of black mud left in the air slowly turns grey-brown as the iron sulfide oxidises.
  • Link to blue carbon: decay is slow without oxygen, which is why so much carbon builds up in mangrove soil.

What makes a true mangrove?

Not vivipary. The standard criteria (Tomlinson 1986, The Botany of Mangroves) are that a true, or "exclusive," mangrove:

  1. Grows only in mangrove habitat: it lives in the intertidal zone and does not spread into dry-land forest. This is the key criterion.
  2. Plays a major role in the community: it can form pure stands and shapes the forest's structure.
  3. Has structural adaptations: such as aerial roots (stilt roots, knee roots, pneumatophores) or vivipary.
  4. Has a way of dealing with salt: salt exclusion at the roots (e.g., Rhizophora) or salt secretion from the leaves (e.g., Avicennia).
  5. Is taxonomically distinct from dry-land relatives, usually at least at genus level.

Mangrove associates (Bebaru, Pandan Laut, Gambir Laut and Tuba Laut on the trail, and Ketapang around Ecocare) grow in mangroves but are not restricted to them, and are equally at home on beaches, riverbanks or inland. Pandan laut even has stilt roots but is still an associate, because it fails criterion 1.

Lists vary between authors. Borderline species such as buta-buta and dungun laut, which also tolerate less salty, more landward conditions, are sometimes called "minor" true mangroves. If asked, the short answer is: "it lives only in the mangrove."

Safety reminders for the kids

  • Don't eat any fruits or seeds.
  • Watch out for pandan laut thorns.
  • Kids with asthma may react to akasia pollen.
  • Don't step on knee roots, pencil roots or pull up propagules.
  • Don't touch buta-buta. Its milky latex irritates skin and, in severe cases, can cause blindness in the eyes. Wash hands if anyone touches it, and never rub eyes.
  • Tuba laut: don't crush the roots or stems, and never put any part of it into water. Wash hands after handling.
  • Nyireh bunga: the woody fruits are the size of a grapefruit or small pomelo, so don't stand right under a fruiting tree.
  • Ketapang: biting and stinging ants can live in its hollow twigs, so don't break the twigs.

For future guides and interns: things said on tours that no source backs up yet. Treat them with healthy scepticism, check before you repeat them, and update this list when you confirm or disprove one.

  • "Akar jangkang paling atas ialah tanda aras air pasang paling tinggi yang pokok ni pernah kena seumur hidup dia." (The topmost stilt root, or akar jangkang, marks the highest tide the tree has ever experienced.) We found no source that supports this, and the published facts about stilt roots point the other way. In Rhizophora, stilt roots grow out from the main stem and down into the mud, working like flying buttresses that hold the tree up in soft, flooded ground (Méndez-Alonzo et al. 2015, Annals of Botany, a study of the close relative R. mangle; PROSEA). In Bakau Minyak they start up to 5 m up the stem (NParks). So stilt roots are supports, and the height of the top one shows where it sprouted on the trunk, not how high the water reached. Status: not used in the tour notes.
  • "Floating nipah islands move with each tide." Partly confirmed. The island seen from the Ecocare jetty changes position from day to day and sometimes drifts far away (observed Oct–Nov 2026; photos from the same spot on 27 Oct and 16 Nov 2026 are in the Nipah section). Whether it moves with each tide, rather than mainly with river flow and wind, has not been checked. To check: note where the island is at high tide and at low tide on the same day.
  • "Dungun twigs were used locally as kayu sugi." Unconfirmed. Sources confirm the twigs are used as toothbrushes, but not where (Useful Tropical Plants; NParks). To check: ask older people in Kertih or Dungun, or find a Malaysian ethnobotany source.
